# NEW BRANCH: Accessibility Technology
- id: accessibility
- era: integrated
- summary: Machines that let people with disabilities read, hear, speak and use computers. It runs from text telephones for deaf callers and reading machines for the blind to the screen readers built into every phone and the rules that make the web usable without sight.
- why: It is a real line of descent with its own inventors, and much of it is software the tree already depends on: screen readers read through the accessibility APIs of Windows, macOS and iOS, and WCAG shapes how the web is built. Related nodes already in the tree: `teletype` (the TTY reused teleprinters), `mac-os-x` (VoiceOver arrived in Tiger), `windows-2000` (first Windows with Narrator), `web` (WCAG), `pdp-11` and the DEC line (DECtalk).

## Optacon
- id: optacon
- year: 1971
- kind: device
- era: integrated
- parent: access-reading-speech
- creators: John Linvill
- wikipedia: Optacon
- summary: The OPtical to TActile CONverter let blind people read ordinary print: a small camera scanned the line and a pad of vibrating pins drew each letter under a fingertip. John Linvill of Stanford conceived it and developed it with SRI, and Telesensory Systems made it from 1971 to 1996.
- sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optacon
- confidence: high

## Kurzweil Reading Machine
- id: kurzweil-reading-machine
- year: 1976
- kind: machine
- era: micro
- parent: access-reading-speech
- creators: Ray Kurzweil
- wikipedia: Ray Kurzweil
- summary: The first machine to read printed books aloud, unveiled on 13 January 1976 with the National Federation of the Blind. It combined Kurzweil Computer Products' omni-font character recognition, a flatbed CCD scanner and a text-to-speech synthesizer, and it filled a tabletop.
- sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Kurzweil
- confidence: high
- note: Wikipedia has no separate article for the machine; `Kurzweil Reading Machine` returns missing and `Kurzweil Computer Products` redirects to `Ray Kurzweil`. Store `Ray Kurzweil` as the title.

- year: 1964
- kind: device

- creators: Robert Weitbrecht, James C. Marsters, Andrew Saks
- wikipedia: Telecommunications device for the deaf
- summary: A teleprinter that types over an ordinary phone line, so deaf people could make calls. In 1964 the deaf physicist Robert Weitbrecht, James Marsters and Andrew Saks founded APCOM to build an acoustic coupler, and they put old military teleprinters back to work with it.

- era: micro
- parent: access-deaf-text
- creators: none (PBS, National Bureau of Standards, National Captioning Institute)
- wikipedia: Closed captioning
- summary: Text of a programme's sound carried hidden in line 21 of the television signal and shown only by a decoder. First demonstrated in December 1971, given line 21 by the FCC in 1976, and broadcast on a regular schedule in the United States from 16 March 1980.

## JAWS
- id: jaws
- year: 1989
- kind: concept
- era: personal
- parent: ibm-screen-reader
- creators: Ted Henter, Rex Skipper
- wikipedia: JAWS (screen reader)
- summary: Job Access With Speech, first released for DOS in 1989 by Ted Henter, who had lost his sight in a car accident. JAWS for Windows 1.0 followed in January 1995, and it is still the most used desktop screen reader in WebAIM's surveys.
- sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JAWS_(screen_reader)
- confidence: high
- note: parent is the earlier screen reader as the class origin; the article does not say JAWS was derived from IBM's code. If the curator wants strict descent, use `screen-readers`.

## NVDA
- id: nvda
- year: 2006
- kind: concept
- era: modern
- parent: jaws
- creators: Michael Curran
- wikipedia: NonVisual Desktop Access
- summary: NonVisual Desktop Access, a free and open source screen reader for Windows written in Python, started by Michael Curran in April 2006 because commercial screen readers cost too much. It is now the second most used screen reader and especially common in developing countries.
- sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NonVisual_Desktop_Access
- confidence: high

## VoiceOver
- id: voiceover
- year: 2005
- kind: concept
- era: modern
- parent: screen-readers
- creators: none (Apple)
- wikipedia: VoiceOver
- summary: Apple's screen reader, built into Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger in April 2005 and added to the iPhone 3GS in June 2009, where it let blind users explore a touch screen by sliding a finger over it. It made a screen reader part of the operating system rather than an expensive extra.
