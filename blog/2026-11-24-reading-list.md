Six pieces from the past week. Most of them look back decades to explain something happening now.

## AI helps uncover critical flaw in ubiquitous internet software

*Doug Jacobson, on The Conversation, 18 November*

A security researcher explains a newly found flaw in the Base64 decoder of Oracle's [Java](node:java) runtime, and why it matters so widely. He starts with why Java spread in the first place: "Java solved that headache with a clever piece of software called the Java Virtual Machine." Quoted under CC BY-ND 4.0. [Read it on The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/ai-helps-uncover-critical-flaw-in-ubiquitous-internet-software-296930)

## AI Is Giving Optical Computing Another Shot

*Dina Genkina, on IEEE Spectrum, 17 November*

Computing with light is more than 70 years old as an idea. This long piece starts from Joseph Goodman's 1978 design for multiplying a matrix by a vector with lenses and follows it to the startups now selling it as a cheaper way to run AI, with honest caveats about where the energy savings vanish. [Read it on IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/optical-computing-ai)

## Why a Computer Scientist Thinks Superintelligence Won't Happen

*Cal Newport, on IEEE Spectrum, 19 November*

![Alan Turing as a teenager, in a suit and tie](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a1/Alan_Turing_Aged_16.jpg "Photo: possibly Arthur Reginald Chaffin, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons")

Newport traces the idea of an intelligence explosion from I. J. Good's 1965 paper to today's labs, then argues against it with the long history behind [backpropagation](node:backpropagation) and Turing's proof that most problems have no algorithm at all. [Read it on IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-superintelligence-debate)

## What Happened to Silicon Valley?

*Tim O'Reilly, on O'Reilly Radar, 20 November*

![Tim O'Reilly smiling, in a striped shirt](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Tim_O%27Reilly_-_2017_%2838700700672%29_%28cropped%29.jpg/960px-Tim_O%27Reilly_-_2017_%2838700700672%29_%28cropped%29.jpg "Photo: Christopher Michel, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons")

The text of a lecture by someone who was there: the early commercial [web](node:web), the 1993 Global Network Navigator and the Web 2.0 years, and then his account of how the industry moved from creating value to extracting it. Long, personal and full of history. [Read it on O'Reilly Radar](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/what-happened-to-silicon-valley/)

## Ladybird is now in alpha

*Andreas Kling, on the Ladybird blog, 23 November*

A short announcement with a long story behind it: the first public build of a browser whose engine is written from scratch rather than forked from the engines behind [Chrome](node:chrome), Safari or [Firefox](node:mozilla-firefox). Worth reading for what an alpha of a new engine promises and what it does not. [Read it on ladybird.org](https://ladybird.org/posts/alpha/)

## Stalwart is now a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange

*Stalwart Labs, on the Stalwart blog, 23 November*

A release post that doubles as a history of the corporate mail server. It explains how on-premises Exchange is being wound down and why that pushes mail toward the cloud, then lists the Exchange protocols an open source server now speaks alongside [IMAP](node:imap) and [SMTP](node:smtp). [Read it on stalw.art](https://stalw.art/blog/microsoft-exchange-replacement/)
